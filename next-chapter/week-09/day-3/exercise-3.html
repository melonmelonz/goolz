Pricing Exercise: Scope + Estimate + Price

Exercise 3 / 4 . Level 1-3 . 40 min

Five steps: scope, estimate, market research, value calculation, price. The order matters. I cannot price First 90 honestly until I know what it does, how long it took, what Renee could buy instead, and what it is worth to her program. Running the steps in order is the discipline. The number at the end is not the hard part. Knowing the ceiling before you quote a number is the hard part.

Step 1 -- Scope

What First 90 does

  • Accepts a case manager's free-text intake note over a webhook (submitted from whatever intake form the program already uses)
  • Uses an AI step to extract a structured record: resident name, appointment type, next date, urgency flag, and a validity check
  • Normalizes the date to a consistent format so reminders can be reliably scheduled
  • Routes urgent and court-date appointments to immediate SMS + Slack notification to the on-call case manager
  • Logs all records to the program's Google Sheet with a drafted one-line reminder for the resident
  • Queues normal records for the Monday digest
  • Flags and surfaces records that fail the guard (bad data, garbled date) by email, so failures are visible and not silent

What it does NOT do

  • Does not replace the case manager's judgment -- it is a routing and logging tool, not a decision-maker
  • Gives no legal or medical advice
  • Does not include a resident-facing portal in this version -- residents receive SMS reminders, that is all
  • Does not integrate with court systems, parole databases, or any external government data
  • Does not send automated reminders on a schedule in this version -- that is a named future scope item

What the client provides

  • Their Google Sheet (I work with their existing structure or create a clean one during setup)
  • A Twilio account and number (I can help them set it up; they pay the Twilio bill directly)
  • A Slack workspace where the case managers already operate
  • Their intake form (whatever they currently use -- I connect the webhook to it)
  • The resource documents for the RAG layer, if they want that added
Writing what it does NOT do is not modesty -- it is protection

The scope section that matters most is the "does not" list. Renee's imagination of "the AI automation" probably includes things I have not built -- a resident portal, automatic court reminders, integration with the parole system. If I do not put those in writing as out-of-scope, the conversation about them will happen after I have taken the money. The "does not" list is the scope wall I am holding. Everything inside it is what I am delivering. Everything outside it is a future contract.

The Bonterra / Apricot finding matters

The fact that the enterprise solution for this space does not publish its pricing is relevant context for my client. It means the well-funded option is inaccessible to a two-person team that cannot afford a multi-month procurement process. The automation I am selling is not competing with CharityTracker on features. It is competing with a spreadsheet on reliability and time. That is a different conversation, and it informs the price.

Step 4 -- Value Calculation

  Time saved per week:
    - Manual data entry from intake notes: ~2 hrs/wk
    - Chasing down appointment records: ~1 hr/wk
    - Composing and sending SMS reminders manually: ~1 hr/wk
    - Sorting urgent vs. normal at the start of each day: ~2 hrs/wk
    -----------------------------------------------
    Total: ~6 hrs/wk saved across both case managers

  Loaded rate for a case manager at a small nonprofit: ~$25/hr
  (includes salary, benefits, overhead; conservative)

  Weekly value: 6 hrs x $25 = $150/wk

  Annual value: $150 x 52 = $7,800/yr

  Uncounted value:
    A missed court date is not a calendar error. It is a parole violation.
    For a resident 90 days out, it can mean reincarceration. That risk is
    not quantifiable, but it is real, and it is the actual thing this
    automation is guarding against. I am not going to put a dollar figure
    on it. I am noting that it exists, because it is the reason "reliable"
    matters more than "convenient" for this particular client.

  Formula: 15-20% of annual value
    15% of $7,800 = $1,170
    20% of $7,800 = $1,560
    Range: $1,170 - $1,560 one-time

  My cost floor: 8h x $75/hr = $600
  (I am pricing below market rate because I am early in this work
   and building a portfolio; $75/hr is already a discount)

Step 5 -- The Price, and the Tension Around It

The formula says somewhere between $1,170 and $1,560. My cost floor is $600. I have room. And I am not charging $1,400.

Here is how I thought through it. The first instinct was the old one: $250, maybe $300, because "it only took a few days" and "they are a nonprofit" and "I do not want to scare them." I have priced things from fear before. The number always came out too low, and the reason was always some version of "I was not sure I was worth more." That is not a pricing method. It is an anxiety.

So I ran the formula first, all the way through, before I let myself name a number. The formula said $1,170-$1,560. That is the ceiling -- what this is worth to Renee in recovered time and reduced risk per year. I am not obligated to charge the ceiling. Value-based pricing tells me the ceiling so I can make a deliberate choice about where to land, not so I am commanded to extract maximum value from a two-person nonprofit serving people who just got out of prison.

My actual price: $900 one-time + $75 per month.

The reasoning I can state out loud: $900 is 11.5% of annual value -- inside the range, on the lower end. The monthly fee covers monitoring, the Twilio pass-through, and up to a couple of small adjustments per month -- it also means I stay attached to the thing and Renee can call me when something changes. I am choosing to charge less than the ceiling because this is my own community, because I believe in the work, and because a $900 invoice is achievable for a small nonprofit in a way that $1,400 might not be. Those are real business reasons, not fear. I can say them to Renee's face.

The key distinction

Charging less ON PURPOSE -- knowing the ceiling, choosing a lower number for stated reasons -- is a business decision. Charging less out of FEAR -- never running the formula, quoting low because you assume any real number will scare the client -- is not pricing. It is self-doubt with a dollar sign. The formula does not command you to charge the maximum. It tells you what the maximum is, so that wherever you land, you know you chose it.
