Day 1 -- Business Thinking / Market Gaps

NCD . WK9 . D1 . four exercises loaded . the week I stop shipping code and start asking who would actually pay for it

Phase 2 is over. I shipped ReEntry Tech on time, walked it through the gate, got my questions from the panel, and -- here is the part that still catches me a little -- it worked. Not perfectly, but it worked. A real URL, on a real phone, for the person I was building it for. Phase 2's entire job was to prove I could build, and I proved it. Eight weeks of building up to that, and now the program pivots, and the pivot is sudden enough that today felt a little disorienting in a good way: the thing I have been rewarded for doing is no longer the main thing.

Phase 3 is called Get Paid with AI, which is a deliberately blunt name. It is not "explore entrepreneurship" or "consider business concepts." It is: someone is going to pay you for this, and today we are going to figure out who, and why, and how much. The morning lecture was about people, not code -- who has a problem real enough that it keeps them up at night, who has budget or can find it, and where those two circles overlap into the narrow target that is actually worth building for. I have been thinking in terms of features and edge cases for months. Today the unit of analysis was a specific human being with a specific frustration and a specific pile of money that they would, under the right conditions, hand over to make the frustration stop.

I brought three businesses into today's exercises. Two of them came out the other side. One did not. The one that did not was a good stress-test casualty -- the gap I thought was a gap already has a name and a price and a free tier, and knowing that fast is the whole point of the research exercises. The one I am carrying forward is the one closest to home. A small reentry nonprofit, two case managers, thirty residents, a shared Google Sheet, and a genuinely bad outcome waiting in any dropped date. I know that world. I came through a program like it. The problem is not abstract to me, which is either a credential or a blind spot, and today helped me figure out which.

Standup

What I'm bringing from Phase 2: a shipped app, a working deployment, and -- more useful -- the muscle memory of scoping something down to what actually matters for a specific person and shipping it. Also: ReEntry Tech is directly adjacent to today's strongest idea. The world that app lives in is the same world I am about to try to sell into. That feels less like luck and more like a thread.

What's on my mind for Phase 3: I am confident in the technical side. What I am less confident in is the pitch -- the thing where I say a number out loud and defend it. I can build a reminder system in a week. I am less sure I can charge what it is actually worth without underselling it because of who the client is. That tension surfaced early today and I did not fully resolve it, which is probably fine; it seems like a Day 3 problem by design.

One thing I want to be careful about: privacy. The businesses I am researching belong to real people. I am keeping everything in these notes at the role-and-first-name level -- no org names, no identifying details. It is the same principle I try to apply when I talk about people from my own time inside: if I would not be fine having it public, it does not go in the notes.

Lecture Notes -- Who Has Problems

The lecture opened with a question that sounds obvious until you actually sit with it: who has a problem? Not who has a general frustration. Not who would use a thing if it were free. Who has a problem that is real, painful, and attached to a budget.

Three filters, and all three have to pass:

FilterThe QuestionThe Tell
RealIs this actually happening, or are we imagining it?You can point to a specific person doing a specific thing the hard way right now
PainfulDoes it hurt enough that someone will change their behavior?They have already tried to fix it -- spreadsheets, workarounds, duct tape
BudgetIs there money attached, or is this a nice-to-have?The pain is costing them money, time, or risk already; the budget exists, it is just going somewhere worse

The insight that hit: budget is not always a line item labeled "software." Sometimes it is six hours of a case manager's week that she is spending on manual text reminders. That is a budget. It is being paid in her time and her burnout. The question is whether you can make the same outcome happen cheaper.

Lecture Notes -- The Sweet Spot

The sweet spot is a three-circle Venn: people have the problem, people have the budget, and I can build the solution. All three. Remove any one of them and you have a hobby, a charity, or a pipe dream, respectively.

The part I had not thought hard about before today: the third circle is not just "can I technically build it." It is also "do I have access to the people who have the problem, and do they have a reason to trust me." Technical ability gets you maybe halfway through the door. The warm contact -- the fact that I know this world from the inside -- is the other half. A solo 3D-scan operator with a half-day of admin per job is a technically easy problem, but I am not in that room. The nonprofit case manager is a much harder technical problem but I have lived context and a warm contact, which changes the calculus entirely.

Lecture Notes -- Value-Based vs Hourly Thinking

Hourly thinking: you count the hours it takes to build the thing, multiply by a rate, and that is your price. The problem is that it disconnects the price from the reason the client cares. If I build a reminder system in eight hours and charge $80/hr, that is $640. But if that reminder system prevents a parole violation, the value to the person and his case manager is not $640. It is incalculable in one direction and very large in another.

Pricing FrameFormulaWhat You're Anchoring To
Hourlyhours × rateyour cost
Value-basedfraction of the value deliveredthe client's outcome

The worked example from the lecture: a 6-hour-per-week manual process, automated. At $25/hr equivalent staff time, that is $150/week, ~$7,800/year. Value-based pricing would say: charge somewhere between "what they currently pay in time" and "what a fraction of a year of that time is worth." A one-time $900 is not even 12 weeks of the problem. That is the frame.

The caveat I kept coming back to: value tells you the ceiling, not the floor you must charge. The ceiling on a prevented parole violation is not a number I can set a price against. The ceiling on a case manager's burnout is not either. Knowing the ceiling is useful -- it stops you from charging $150 for something worth $7,800. But it does not mean you are obligated to charge everything the ceiling allows, especially when the client is a two-person nonprofit running on grants.

Lecture Notes -- Person, Problem, Price

The simplest possible framing for a service business:

ElementWhat it isFor Idea A
Persona specific human being, not a market segmentRenee, case manager at a small reentry nonprofit, two staff, ~30 residents
Problemwhat they do the hard way right nowtracking every resident's parole dates, court dates, cert deadlines in a shared sheet, texting reminders by hand the night before
Pricewhat it costs them now vs. what you'll charge6-8 hrs/week of manual work + low-grade fear of a dropped date vs. $900 one-time + $75/mo

The version of this that sounds like a sentence: "I help small reentry programs make sure no resident misses a parole date, by turning the intake spreadsheet they already keep into an automated reminder system."

Exercises

  • Exercise 1 Identify 3 Real Businesses map three real businesses against the person / pain / evidence / solution / budget framework, research what tools already exist and roughly what they charge, and pick the strongest
  • Exercise 2 Stress-Test Each Other's Ideas breakout-room feedback on all three ideas: is it real, is it painful enough, can you build it in a week, what would you charge -- drop, park, or keep each one and name a strongest
  • Exercise 3 Competitor Research + Pricing for Idea A: map the real competitors, what they charge (and where pricing is opaque, say so), find the gap, and write a one-paragraph positioning pitch
  • Exercise 4 Problem-Solution-Price Map fill the full map for Idea A: who has the problem, how painful, how they solve it today, what I'd build, what tools, how long, what to charge -- and be honest about where the numbers come from
Verbal Mini-Demo -- 90 Seconds, Out Loud

My partner gave me 90 seconds to pitch Idea A. I said: There is a small reentry nonprofit in my city -- two case managers, about thirty residents. Right now the case manager I know, Renee, tracks every resident's parole check-in, court date, job application, and certification deadline in one shared Google Sheet, and she texts the reminders by hand the night before. That is six to eight hours of her week, every week. A missed parole check-in is not an inconvenience -- it can be a violation, and a violation can send someone back inside. I want to build her a pipeline: resident intake goes into a form, AI pulls the structured fields out of the messy free-text note, the record goes cleanly into the sheet, and automated SMS reminders go out the day before each deadline plus a Monday digest of the week's schedule. One-time setup fee of $900, with a $75/month option if she wants me keeping an eye on it.

My partner's question landed where I was afraid it would: "Is the $900 sustainable -- or are you underpricing it because it's your community?"

My honest answer was: both. The formula says more like $1,200 to $1,500 based on the time savings. I landed at $900 deliberately, because the client is a grant-funded nonprofit and I know what those budgets look like, and because there is a version of this where charging market rate for something in your own community feels like a different kind of wrong. That is a real tension and I am not pretending it is not. I said I would think harder about it on Day 3, which is apparently exactly when the pricing session happens, so I guess the curriculum knows.

The Thing That Clicked Today

The value-based pricing reframe is not subtle, but it hit me harder than I expected when I ran it against a real example. The reminder system is not worth $250 because it took me a few hours to build. It is worth what a prevented violation is worth to a man who just came home and the case manager who stayed late to text him. That number does not have a clean formula but it is certainly not $250. Value-based thinking is what closes that gap -- it forces you to anchor the price to the outcome instead of to your cost.

But the same insight cuts the other direction too, and nobody in the lecture made quite enough of this: value tells you the ceiling, not the price you must charge. Knowing the ceiling is essential -- without it you would charge $250 for something worth $7,800, and you would do the client a weird kind of disservice by making the work seem cheap and disposable. But the ceiling is not the price. The price is a judgment call that involves the ceiling, the client's real budget, the relationship, and what kind of business you want to run. I have been in rooms where a ceiling was used to extract every possible dollar from people who did not have much leverage, and I do not want to build that muscle. The thing that clicked is that value-based pricing is a tool, not an obligation, and the day I confuse one for the other is the day I stop being the person I am trying to be.
