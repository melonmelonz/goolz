Day 3 -- Go Live: Ship To Real Users

NCD . WK8 . D3 . five exercises loaded . the day someone who is not me opens my thing

The app is on the internet already. I deployed it yesterday, watched the live URL serve the empty state on a phone that was not mine, and felt the specific vertigo of a thing that exists outside my laptop. So today is not a deploy day, exactly -- it is the day I make the deployed thing real for a person. The build week has been one long argument with myself about what counts, and there is one Must left standing on the list, the biggest one: documented user activity. Two to three real people, on the live URL, with screenshots and a sentence of feedback each. That is the gate requirement I have been circling for a week, and circling because it is the part I cannot fake. I can convince myself the code works. I cannot convince myself a stranger used it.

The morning is the unglamorous half. Yesterday's "test like a stranger" click-through surfaced three bugs that I never would have found by reading the code -- a long AI summary that blows past the bottom of its card, a deadline that renders as a raw ISO string the second you load it on a phone, and a flash of the old list right after a delete. None of them crash anything. All of them make the app look like a student project instead of a thing. So I triage them, I fix them, I do one polish should-have, and then I do the cleanup nobody sees: strip the leftover console.logs, finally kill the Week 4 placeholder tagline, and check the layout at 360px where my user's phone actually lives.

The afternoon is the part I had been avoiding. First the production hardening -- a favicon, OpenGraph and Twitter meta tags so the link previews like a real site when it lands as a text message, and a privacy-respecting analytics snippet so "documented user activity" has data underneath it and not just my memory of it. Then the real thing: I send the link to the guy who came home in March, I watch him use it without coaching, I get two more people on it, and I capture the evidence before it evaporates. And at the very end, the question that the whole week has been building toward and that I am not allowed to answer with a feeling: is it actually done? Four criteria, a proof next to each. Not at 11pm, not on vibes.

Lecture Notes -- Bugs From Testing, Triaged

The three bugs from yesterday's click-through are all P2 or P3 -- none of them crash the app, which is exactly why I would never have caught them by reading the route handlers. You only find them by clicking like a person.

BugWhat happensPriority
AI summary overflows the carda long summary runs past the bottom edge of the card and bleeds over the row below itP2
Deadline renders as raw ISOthe forklift cert deadline shows as 2026-07-14T00:00:00.000Z on mobile instead of a date a human readsP2
Flash of old list after deleteoptimistic delete removes the card, then the server response briefly re-paints the old list before settlingP3

The deadline one stings the most, because it is the field my user actually leans on -- a parole date, a cert deadline. Showing him T00:00:00.000Z is showing him that I never once looked at his data on a phone.

Lecture Notes -- The Last-Mile Cleanup

This is the small, invisible work that separates "a student project" from "a thing." Nobody sees the cleanup. They feel it. Three items: kill the console.log calls still sitting in the AI route, replace the Week 4 placeholder tagline that still reads "your next chapter starts here" with the real pitch, and pull the live URL up at 360px -- the actual width of a cheap Android, which is the actual phone my user has -- and fix whatever breaks. On Day 1 I logged the filter chips wrapping into a ragged stack below 380px; 360px is where I confirm it and fix it.

The console.logs are the tell. A debug console.log(summary) shipping to production is the software equivalent of leaving the price tag on. It does no harm and it announces that nobody did a final pass.

Lecture Notes -- Production Hardening

Three things turn a deployed URL into something that reads as a real site. A favicon, so the browser tab is not a blank page icon. OpenGraph and Twitter meta tags, so when I text the link to my user it unfurls into a card with a title and a description instead of a naked blue URL. And a lightweight, privacy-respecting analytics snippet, so "documented user activity" has data behind it -- page views, not just my screenshots.

The OG tags matter more than they look like they do. My user is going to receive this app as a text message. A link that previews as a real site with a name and a one-line description is the difference between "this is a tool a person made for me" and "this looks like a scam, I'm not tapping that." For a reentry app, the shared link is often the entire first impression.

Lecture Notes -- Documented User Activity

This is the gate Must, and it is the one I cannot do alone at my desk. The standard is concrete: two to three real people open the live URL, I watch them use it without coaching them, and I capture a screenshot plus one sentence of feedback per person. The watching is the point. A real user does things I never planned for -- and the first thing I learn is usually a usability finding I would have argued against if a classmate had suggested it.

The discipline here is to split the work in two: use it, then capture it. If I let myself just enjoy watching my user succeed, the evidence evaporates -- I will remember it went well and I will have nothing to show the gate. So the screenshot and the feedback sentence get captured in the moment, every time, before I let myself feel good about it.

Lecture Notes -- Is It Actually Done?

"Done" is a claim, and a claim needs evidence. Four criteria, and next to each one a proof I can point at -- not a feeling I have late at night that it is probably fine.

CriterionThe proof
It worksI clicked every feature on the live URL -- create, edit, delete, filter, search, AI summary -- and observed each one
It's deployedthe real Vercel URL loads over HTTPS on a phone that is not mine
A real person used itscreenshots exist of three people using it, with a feedback sentence each
I can explain ita one-paragraph walkthrough is drafted -- what it does, how I built it, what AI I used

Every line has a proof or it is not checked. "I think the edit works" is not a check. "I edited a card on the live URL and watched the board update" is a check.

Verbal Mini-Demo -- What Watching A Real User Taught Me

Two minutes to my partner, not on what I built but on what I learned watching someone use it. The headline finding: my user tried to tap the whole card to open an opportunity, the way you tap anything on a phone, and the edit affordance was a small button tucked in the corner that he never reached for. He sat there for a second, a little confused, and that second told me more than every test I had written. He was not wrong. The card looks tappable, so it should be tappable. I had built the interaction the way it was easy to build, not the way a person expects it to behave. The point of the demo is that this is not a bug I could have reasoned my way to. It only exists because a person who is not me put a thumb on the screen.

The other thing I name is the feeling, because it is part of the lesson: the specific terror of handing the link over and then having to sit on my hands and not narrate. Every instinct said to lean in and explain. The whole value was in not doing that -- in letting the confusion happen so I could see it. Partner challenge: "what is the one thing you changed, or wanted to change, because of what you watched -- and would you have ever found it by yourself?" The honest answer for me is no, I would not have, and that is the entire argument for getting a real user on it before the gate instead of after.

The Thing That Clicked Today

For the whole build week I have been treating "done" as a feeling I was waiting to arrive -- some moment late at night where I would lean back and just know the thing was finished. It never came, and today I understood why: it is not a feeling, it is a claim, and a claim is something you back with evidence. The four-criteria checklist did to "done" exactly what the Day 1 audit did to my bug list. It dragged a vague internal state into a row with a proof next to it. "I think a real person can use it" is a feeling. "Here are three screenshots of three people using it" is a fact. One of those survives the gate and one does not, and I had been planning to walk in with the feeling.

The deeper thing is that the evidence is not just for the gate. Watching my user reach for the card instead of the edit button, watching the deadline finally render as a date he could read instead of an ISO string -- that is the thing the whole project was supposed to be about, and I almost shipped it without ever seeing it happen. A reentry app is not a portfolio piece. Somebody is leaning on it for a forklift cert deadline and a parole date. The discipline of capturing evidence turned out to be the discipline of actually looking at the person I built it for, instead of looking at my own code and assuming it reached him. It did, mostly. But I only know that because I watched, and I only have proof because I captured it before I let myself feel good.
