Telling The AI-Touch Story

Exercise 3 / 5 . Level 2 . 30 min

Goal: a tight 90-second account of the AI feature -- what it does, the prompt I send, the shape I ask back, what I keep versus throw away, and the thing that actually makes it work, which is the tests. The AI-touch is the headline of the whole project, so this story has to be the most rehearsed ninety seconds I have. The trap is to spend it gushing about how clever the AI is. The gate does not care that the AI is clever. It cares whether I understood what I was doing when I wired a non-deterministic thing into an app a person depends on.

What it does, in one breath

My user gets a job posting as a wall of text -- requirements, benefits boilerplate, equal-opportunity legalese, all of it -- and reading it is work. He pastes the URL. The app fetches the posting text and sends it to OpenAI with a prompt that asks for a specific shape: three plain-language sections, "what this is / what they want / what to bring." Out comes three short readable blocks instead of eight hundred words. That is the entire feature, and it is aimed squarely at the real moment: a guy who came home in March, looking at a forklift-adjacent warehouse posting, who needs to know in ten seconds whether it is worth his afternoon.

The prompt and the shape

The prompt does two jobs: it sets the audience and it pins the output shape. Plain language, no jargon, three labeled sections, short. Asking for a fixed shape is what makes the output testable later -- if I let the model freeform, there is nothing to assert against. 

// app/api/summarize/route.js -- the prompt asks for a fixed shape
const prompt = `Summarize this job posting for someone re-entering the
workforce. Plain language, no HR jargon. Return exactly three short
sections, labeled:

WHAT THIS IS:
WHAT THEY WANT:
WHAT TO BRING:

Posting:
${postingText}`;

What I kept vs what I rejected

The model does not just hand back the three sections. It editorializes -- adds an upbeat closing line, pads with "this is a great opportunity," sometimes invents a benefit the posting never mentioned. I keep the three sections and reject the rest. The keep/reject is the engineering decision, and it is the part of the story that shows I treated the model as a component I direct, not an oracle I obey.

Model gave backKept or rejected
The three labeled sectionskept -- it's the whole point
"This is a fantastic opportunity for growth!"rejected -- cheerleading my user didn't ask for
A benefit not in the postingrejected hard -- inventing facts is the dangerous failure for this user
A long preamble before the sectionsrejected -- I parse to the labels and drop everything before

Why I can trust it -- the tests

Here is the spine of the story. The Vitest suite from Week 7 is the reason I can put a thing I do not control in front of a person who is leaning on it. The tests pin the contract: the response parses into exactly the three labeled sections, missing sections are handled instead of crashing, a malformed or empty model response degrades to a friendly message rather than rendering garbage. The OpenAI call itself is mocked, so the tests assert my handling of the output, which is the part I own and the part that has to be reliable. 

// summarize.test.js -- the tests pin the shape, OpenAI mocked
it("parses the three labeled sections from a well-formed response", () => {
  const out = parseSummary(SAMPLE_GOOD);
  expect(out.whatThisIs).toBeTruthy();
  expect(out.whatTheyWant).toBeTruthy();
  expect(out.whatToBring).toBeTruthy();
});

it("degrades to a friendly message when the model returns garbage", () => {
  const out = parseSummary("...unstructured rambling, no labels...");
  expect(out.error).toMatch(/couldn't summarize/i);
  // never renders raw garbage to the user
});
The strongest version of the story is the part where I didn't trust it

My best 90-second material is not "the AI is great." It is the moment I distrusted the model and a test caught the drift before my user ever saw it. I wired in a non-deterministic component and the way I made it safe for a person who leans on it was the suite -- pinning the shape, handling the garbage path, degrading instead of rendering nonsense. The AI is the demo. The tests are the engineering, and the honest, gate-winning version of the story leads with the distrust.

Deliverable

A 90-second AI-touch story I can deliver tight: the real moment (wall-of-text posting, ten seconds to decide), the prompt and the three-section shape, the keep/reject decisions with inventing-a-benefit called out as the dangerous one, and the tests as the reason an uncontrolled output is safe to ship. Rehearsed and timed -- it runs at about 80 seconds, which leaves room. The version that leads with "here's where I didn't trust it and the test caught it" is the one I am taking to the gate.
